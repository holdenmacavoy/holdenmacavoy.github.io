Working paper

Health Capital and US Healthcare Spending

The limits of a morbidity-based explanation in a 2019 country comparison.

Holden MacAvoyIndependent researchNovember 2026
The question

Does the U.S. spend so much on health care because its population is sicker and needs more care just to stay where it is?

What I did

Built a simple health-capital model, then compared spending with obesity and diabetes across 37 countries in 2019.

What I found

Neither condition has a clear positive link to spending once income and age are accounted for. The U.S. is the exception, not the pattern.

Why this question

In 2019 the United States spent 16.54% of GDP on health care, against an unweighted average of 8.85% across the 37 countries in my sample. Its life expectancy, 78.8 years, was below the sample average of 80.9. One explanation for spending more and getting less is that Americans start from worse health. If a population is sicker, a lot of medical spending goes to keeping health from slipping further, so spending can be high even when outcomes don't improve.

The U.S. also has the highest obesity rate in the sample, so the idea is plausible on its face. This paper asks whether it holds up across countries.

The framework

I start from Grossman's (1972) idea that health is a stock of capital that wears down over time and can be rebuilt through investment, including medical care. Borrowing the investment-versus-depreciation logic of the Solow model, a representative person's health stock changes like this:

Δht = At st yt − δ(Dt) ht(1)

Here h is health capital, s is health spending as a share of GDP, y is income per person and A converts spending into actual health investment. The depreciation rate δ rises with disease burden D. Holding health steady at a target level h* requires:

stmaint = δ(Dt) h*tAt yt(2)

So a sicker population needs more spending just to stand still, as long as income, the target and the effectiveness of care are fixed. That's a statement about what's required, not a prediction of what countries actually spend. The empirical question is whether real spending lines up with it.

Data and method

Sample
37 countries, 2019 (pre-COVID)
Spending
Current health expenditure, % of GDP
Disease burden
Obesity and diabetes indices, NCD-RisC
Controls
Log GDP per person (PPP), share aged 65+
Estimation
OLS with HC3 robust standard errors
Robustness
Without the U.S.; log spending per person

Each condition gets its own regression, run once on all 37 countries and once without the United States, to see how much the results depend on it. The whole analysis is reproducible from public data in R.

Results

Without any controls, the link between obesity and spending is slightly negative, and it gets more negative when the U.S. is dropped. After adjusting for income and age, none of the estimates show the clear positive relationship the maintenance story needs.

Repeating the analysis with spending per person instead of spending as a share of GDP gives the same direction: roughly +0.3% per point of obesity in the full sample and −0.6% without the U.S., with intervals that include zero.

High disease burden and high spending go together in the United States, but that combination doesn't generalize into a consistent relationship across countries.

What it means

This doesn't mean chronic disease is unimportant. Studies of individuals find that obesity adds substantially to medical costs. The point is narrower: across countries, differences in obesity and diabetes don't explain why U.S. spending is so much higher. Other research points to prices and administrative costs (Papanicolas, Woskie and Jha, 2018) and to the price and intensity of services (Dieleman et al., 2017), which are where I'd look next.

Limits: one year, 37 countries, and model-based disease estimates, so the results are descriptive rather than causal. The U.S. obesity index sits above every other country in the sample, which makes any prediction for the U.S. from the other 36 an extrapolation.

Key references

  • Grossman, M. (1972). On the concept of health capital and the demand for health. Journal of Political Economy.
  • Solow, R. M. (1956). A contribution to the theory of economic growth. Quarterly Journal of Economics.
  • Papanicolas, I., Woskie, L. R., & Jha, A. K. (2018). Health care spending in the United States and other high-income countries. JAMA.
  • Dieleman, J. L., et al. (2017). Factors associated with increases in US health care spending, 1996–2013. JAMA.
  • NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (2024, 2026). Diabetes and obesity estimates. The Lancet; Nature.

Investing

I've been investing for about seven years. Nvidia was my first big win, and since then I've mostly bought and held a small number of companies I've researched myself.

What's worked

NvidiaNVDA
Worked

My first big win, and the stock that got me serious about investing. I held it as data center demand turned it into the center of the AI buildout.

LessonLetting a winner run mattered more than finding the perfect entry.

PalantirPLTR
Worked

I rotated part of my Nvidia gains into Palantir, betting that its software would become how governments and large companies actually put AI to work.

LessonTaking profits from one part of a trend and moving them to another part of it can work as well as adding to what has already run.

AMDAMD
Worked

I bought most of my position in the spring 2025 selloff, when tariff fears hit chip stocks hard, and sold in stages through 2026 as its AI chip business grew.

LessonA broad panic is a good time to buy companies you already understand. Selling in stages beat trying to time one exit.

AlphabetGOOGL
Worked

I bought through 2025 while the market worried that AI chatbots would hurt Google search, and sold in early 2026 after the stock rerated.

LessonWhen a strong business is priced for a disruption that hasn't happened yet, the fear can be the opportunity.

How I value a growth company

For high-growth companies that aren't profitable yet, a single price target hides most of the risk. I build three scenarios and make dilution explicit, since new share issuance can erase a lot of upside.
